An archive of ordinary objects, rooms, interfaces, routines, paperwork, sounds, stories, and the context around them.

We began collecting in 2026 while the context around ordinary things could still be recorded. We don’t yet know which parts of the present will be difficult to explain later.

We collect the things people might never think to preserve.

Why ordinary life?

Much of the present will disappear without leaving a record.

01

The object

The receipt, key, charger, badge, bottle, bag, cable, or handwritten note. The object is evidence; its context belongs in the record too.

02

The context

Where it was kept, how it was used, what surrounded it, and what those details explain.

03

The voice

“I use this every day.” “I hate this thing.” “I don’t know why I still have it.” Ordinary testimony is part of the collection.

Attention is part of the archive

Archives usually preserve what someone thought worth saving. Ordinary life is easier to lose: bedrooms, grocery lists, interfaces, routines, meals, workplaces, conversations, clutter, sounds, habits, and the small arrangements people build around themselves.

Ordinary moments don’t need hidden greatness to belong here. A human life doesn’t have to be exceptional to matter.

To pay attention to ordinary life is to practice seeing people as fully human.

Attention here means looking carefully without turning a record into a verdict. A photograph, room, receipt, or routine can document part of a life. It can’t contain the whole person.

One museum / Local collections

Ordinary life is everywhere. Its stories should be, too.

The Museum may take many local forms. A neighborhood, town, language community, diaspora, workplace, or temporary group can keep its own collection and still be part of the wider Museum.

Communities anywhere may establish collections and exhibitions under their own stewardship. Shared preservation, cataloging, translation, accessibility, and discovery tools can connect them. Local stewards decide what ordinary life means where they are, how records are described, and what remains local, restricted, delayed, or offline. No local collection speaks for an entire city, culture, or community. Several partial, overlapping, and contradictory records may coexist. Ordinary life doesn’t have one representative version.

01

Shared globally

Shared services can include preservation copies, backups, catalog records, consent and embargo handling, translation, accessibility, documentation, and cross-collection search.

02

Shaped locally

Local stewards decide what to collect, which languages to use, how records are described, where physical objects remain, and how stewardship changes hands.

03

Connected by choice

A local museum may operate independently, join a shared exhibition, or make selected catalog records searchable across the network. Ownership, context, and local authority stay with the collection.

It doesn’t need a building

A museum can last for decades or one afternoon. It can be organized around a place, a season, a question, or a single exhibition.

01

Rooted somewhere

An ongoing neighborhood, village, city, or regional collection cared for by people who know the place from within.

02

Here for a while

A pop-up museum, a year-long archive, a public gathering, or an exhibition assembled for one particular moment.

03

Made to move

A traveling collection, a table at a market, a room inside another community space, or a museum carried from place to place.

04

Distributed

A digital, diasporic, language-based, or thematic museum for a community spread across more than one place.

A home within the network

Participating museums may use a recognizable address within the wider Museum while keeping their own name, languages, collections, exhibitions, and stewards.

sf.museumofordinarylife.org kumasi.museumofordinarylife.org dhaka.museumofordinarylife.org mekongdelta.museumofordinarylife.org dine.museumofordinarylife.org

Museum subdomains are optional. A community may use its own domain, keep a collection offline, or connect only selected records. Several museums may overlap in the same place without any one of them becoming the authoritative account of that community.

What belongs in a museum of ordinary life?

Almost anything, if it helps explain how everyday life worked. Common things can matter as much as rare ones. The Museum collects objects and records with their context: what this was, where it was, how it was used, who used it, and when. The collection isn’t looking for exceptional lives. It preserves enough ordinary detail to keep people visible as more than categories or examples.

Field Notes

Ways of noticing

Field note / Photography

Photograph the object twice.

First as an object. Then where it normally lives. A key on a blank surface tells us what a key looked like. A key beside the door, receipts and yesterday’s mail tells us about a life.

Field note / Digital life

Interfaces disappear quickly.

Software changes quickly. Screenshots of payment prompts, settings pages, error messages, and ordinary interfaces may be harder to reconstruct later than a physical object. Record them while they still exist.

Field note / Meaning

Nothing profound is required.

An object doesn’t need a moving story to belong in the Museum. “This is the mug I use every morning” is historical information. So is “I don’t remember why I kept this.”

Field note / Attention

A fragment isn't a whole person.

A record is partial. Document what is there without pretending it explains the person. A room, receipt, routine, or group of objects can show context while the person remains larger and more complicated than the record.

Archive ethics / Data & AI

A record of humanity shouldn’t become a free-for-all dataset.

Once the collection is large enough, it can also function as a dataset about people: how they lived, what they kept, how homes and streets worked, what software looked like, what worried them, and what an ordinary Tuesday felt like.

The collection may be useful for research, art, data analysis, and machine learning. That doesn’t make every use permitted. Public display, research, machine learning, and generative reuse are recorded as separate permissions.

Preservation and publication are separate decisions. A contribution can enter the archive without entering public view. Contributors may choose immediate display, anonymous display, release on or after a date, a delay measured in years, a later request for permission, or archival preservation without public display.

A dataset is still a partial record. It may show patterns, surroundings, or habits, but it doesn’t contain the whole person who contributed it. Search and computation don’t erase the need for human context.

Working principle

You may learn from this, but don’t pretend you lived it.

MOL / DISPLAY

Public display

May this contribution appear publicly in the Museum, an exhibition, publication or public archive? If yes, it doesn’t have to appear immediately.

MOL / EMBARGO

Delayed release

Publish now, publish on or after a chosen date, wait a chosen number of years, ask again later, or preserve it without public display. The archive can keep something before the world is ready to see it.

MOL / RESEARCH

Research

May researchers study the contribution as part of historical, cultural, social or technical research?

MOL / LEARN

Machine learning

May computational systems analyze or learn patterns from the contribution without treating generative reuse as automatically permitted?

MOL / GENERATE

Generative reuse

May a system reproduce, remix, imitate or generate material from the contribution? That’s a separate permission from machine learning.

MOL / CREDIT

Identity & attribution

Should reuse identify the contributor, remain anonymous, or preserve attribution only inside the archive?

MOL / FUTURE

Future uses

If a future use can’t yet be described in concrete terms: allow it, decline it, or require the Museum to ask again.

MOL / WITHDRAW

Change your mind

Contributors may revise permissions or request withdrawal where the Museum can lawfully and technically do so.

MOL / CONTEXT

Context stays attached

Contributor wording, provenance, and stated limits stay with the record and the object.

MOL / RECORD

Consent is history too

A person’s decision to share, restrict, delay, or withhold a record is itself historical evidence.

These permissions govern how the Museum handles material. Some downstream copies, indexes, or completed model training can’t be reversed. When that’s true, the Museum should say so plainly. A delayed-release or archive-only instruction governs public access through the Museum; it doesn’t grant research, machine learning, generative reuse, or other permissions.

Contribute / Opening collection

Send us something boring.

Photograph or record something from your ordinary life in 2026. Boring is exciting. It doesn’t need to look historically important. That’s part of the point.

Your contribution doesn’t need to be tidy, beautiful, representative, or complete. Ordinary life is allowed to look like ordinary life. Share only what you are comfortable preserving. Add as much or as little explanation as feels right.

01 Send the original photo, video, audio file, screenshot, or document when possible. Check first for private information you don’t want preserved.
02 Tell us what it is and when you documented it.
03 Give an approximate location if it matters to the record. A city name is plenty.
04 Add any context you want. One sentence is enough. No explanation is also useful information.
05 Choose when and how it may appear publicly: "publish now with my name" / "publish now anonymously" / "publish on or after [date]" / "wait [X years] before publishing" / "ask me again after [date or X years]" / "archive only and ask before publishing" / "archive only; don't publish."
06 For uses beyond display, choose separately whether to allow research, machine learning, generative reuse, attribution, and uses that can’t yet be described. You can also require the Museum to ask again. These choices may be more restrictive than your publication choice.

Museum information / About

A museum for the things we haven’t lost yet.

Established in 2026, the Museum of Ordinary Life documents everyday life while its objects, routines, interfaces, and explanations are still available. We collect ordinary things together with the context that makes them legible.

What we do

We collect and document ordinary objects, spaces, interfaces, routines, sounds, paperwork, and personal accounts that would otherwise leave little historical record.

The Museum records the thing and its place in a life: where it was kept, how it was used, what surrounded it, and what the person living with it thought about it. The object is evidence; the surrounding life is part of the record.

Why 2026?

Because this is when we started.

Historical collections are often assembled after the fact, when ordinary details have already disappeared. We’re documenting the present before we know what will later seem important, strange, obsolete, beautiful, or difficult to explain.

What counts?

A grocery receipt can count. So can a bus stop, a work badge, a phone notification, a medicine cabinet, a rent notice, a junk drawer, a five-minute recording of a street, or the place somebody charges their phone every night.

Rarity is not a requirement. Neither are beauty or a profound story. A common object may tell a future researcher more than a rare one.

Why ordinary life?

Archives usually preserve what someone thought worth saving. Ordinary life is easier to lose: bedrooms, grocery lists, interfaces, routines, meals, workplaces, conversations, clutter, sounds, habits, and the small arrangements people build around themselves.

Ordinary moments don’t need hidden greatness to belong here. A human life doesn’t have to be exceptional to matter.

To pay attention to ordinary life is to practice seeing people as fully human.

That also means resisting the urge to turn one contribution into a verdict about the person behind it. The archive can preserve evidence of a life without pretending that any object, photograph or description contains the whole person.

What kind of museum is this?

For now, the Museum is a distributed digital archive, not a building. Records can originate anywhere ordinary life is being lived and documented.

The Museum may also take local forms. Communities may create their own local collections, exhibitions and museum structures using shared preservation and cataloging tools while keeping local context and authority.

The Museum borrows methods from public history, participatory archives, and art. Its first obligation is archival: preserve records with enough context that someone else can understand them later, while the people and systems that produced them are still here to describe them.

Related work / neighbors

The Museum exists alongside other projects that take everyday life seriously. One especially close neighbor is the Museum of Everyday Life ↗ in Glover, Vermont, whose philosophy, performances, collections and exhibitions explore people and their relationships to everyday objects.

We discovered their work after establishing the Museum of Ordinary Life in 2026. The similar names were not intentional. The overlap is real and worth acknowledging.

This Museum starts collecting before ordinary context disappears. It keeps objects together with their surroundings, interfaces, routines, documents, sounds, testimony, provenance, permissions, and release dates. The projects overlap, but they aren’t the same project.

Who makes the collection?

Everyone can. Contributors don't need archival training or a theory of why something matters. The person living with an object often knows something about it that a curator never could.

The Museum preserves contributors’ own words wherever possible and distinguishes archival originals from edited public copies.

Who is behind the Museum?

The Museum was established in 2026 and is currently stewarded as an independent experimental cultural project. It’s not yet a conventional museum organization.

The Museum isn’t meant to have a permanent curatorial authority at its center. Its longer-term model is distributed stewardship: people and small stewardship groups care for collections, preservation, access, interpretation and exhibitions while the archive itself remains shared across communities.

Software can handle backups, cataloging, duplication, translation, and access. Questions of context, consent, interpretation, memory, and disagreement remain matters for human judgment.

AI, data & consent

Once the collection is large enough, it also becomes a dataset about human life. Researchers may use it to study details that conventional historical records rarely preserve. Where contributors permit it, the collection may also be used for machine learning.

That possibility doesn’t grant automatic permission. Public display, research, machine learning, generative reuse, attribution, and uses that can’t yet be described are recorded separately. Contributors can say yes to one and no to another. Public display may be immediate, delayed to a date or number of years, made subject to later confirmation, or declined.

A contributor can say: preserve this now, but don’t show it yet. Some records may remain closed for years or permanently. Historical value doesn’t require immediate visibility.

The Museum’s working principle is: You may learn from this, but don’t pretend you lived it. Consent, provenance, and context stay with the object when it becomes data.

What are we trying to preserve?

Context.

The invisible systems underneath an ordinary Tuesday: money, work, housing, technology, transportation, bureaucracy, domestic routines, social rules, waiting, maintenance, care, convenience and inconvenience.

People rarely experience these things as “history” while they are happening. That’s why we’re interested in them.

The record should also leave room for the person: imperfect, adaptive, contradictory, and unfinished. A room, receipt, or routine can document part of a life. It can’t explain the whole life.

The question behind the museum

If someone fifty years from now wanted to understand what it felt like to be alive today, what would we wish we had saved?

Museum information / Stewardship

A museum cared for by more than one person.

The Museum is being built around shared stewardship. Stewards may care for collections, review records, make exhibitions, document ordinary life, maintain the website and preservation systems, and take part in Museum decisions.

An invitation to inaugural stewards

We’re looking for a small first group of people to help establish the Museum’s practices. There’s no finished set of tasks to carry out.

Museum experience is welcome but not required. Archivists, artists, technologists, journalists, librarians, neighborhood historians, careful observers, collectors of strange little things, and anyone attentive to ordinary life may have something to contribute.

Collection

Caring for and describing the collection, reviewing submissions, preserving contributor context, and helping decide what the Museum should collect.

Consent & ethics

Considering privacy, permissions, attribution, delayed release, withdrawal requests, and the difference between preservation, public display, research, machine learning and generative reuse.

Attention & interpretation

Every contribution is a fragment of a person, place, or moment. Stewards should describe what the record supports and stop there; the archive can’t fairly claim more.

The collection documents people; it doesn’t diagnose, rank, explain or reduce them. Description should preserve context without turning ordinary difference, messiness or contradiction into spectacle.

Exhibitions & fieldwork

Developing exhibitions, documenting ordinary environments and routines, and finding ways to help people notice what usually goes unrecorded.

Community

Building relationships with contributors, neighborhoods, local historians, cultural spaces and other communities whose ordinary lives become part of the archive.

Helping communities establish their own local museum structures, preserve culturally specific context, and connect with the wider network only in the ways they choose.

Local museums & shared exhibitions

Supporting place-based, community-based and thematic museums that remain locally shaped while sharing preservation tools, documentation and mutual support.

Exhibitions may be local, collaborative across several communities, or Museum-wide. Each record keeps its provenance and permissions.

Project context / beneath the museum

This museum exists in two times at once.

The Museum of Ordinary Life is a real archive established in 2026. It’s also an institution imagined inside a larger speculative fiction project called No One Noticed.

The larger project

No One Noticed began as a speculative fiction project about a near-future social and technological change that happens gradually enough that the people living through it don’t recognize a single moment when the world changes.

The project now includes a book, websites, archival experiments, art, design, software, and real artifacts made from its fictional future.

Why make the Museum real?

In the fictional future, the Museum is a source on ordinary life before and during that change. The real Museum starts the collection now instead of inventing it later, while the period it may someday describe is still the present.

A photograph made in 2026 can be contemporary documentation, art, historical evidence, and an artifact within the fiction at the same time.

The reality layer

The Museum is the first part of a wider “Reality Layer”: websites, archives, tools, interfaces, documents, and institutions from the imagined future that can be built in the present.

Some will remain fictional. Some may become working prototypes. A few may become useful institutions on their own.

The archive becomes a dataset

Collect enough ordinary life and the archive becomes a machine-readable record of human routines, objects, spaces, and explanations that conventional datasets often miss.

In No One Noticed, future intelligent systems use the Museum to study what people valued, how they lived, and what they chose to preserve. The real Museum sets rules for that use now.

Permissions are part of the record. They document how people wanted their lives represented, studied, and learned from.

Some records may open years after they were contributed. An item documented in 2026 might remain private until 2036, 2046, or later. Over time, the Museum may hold unopened time capsules whose release instructions are part of the archive.

The rule

Blur ontology, not consent.

The project may blur whether an artifact belongs to the present or the future, whether something began as art or institution, and where fiction ends. Safety, privacy, money, identity, emergencies, and official authority remain unambiguous.

Why hide this here?

Because the Museum has to work without the fiction.

A visitor can contribute without knowing the novel exists. The deeper context is here for people who go looking for it.

Working method / prefigurative fiction

Build small pieces of a possible future now. See what happens. Let the result change the fiction.
