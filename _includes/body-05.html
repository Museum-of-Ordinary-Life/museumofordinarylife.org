A proactive one. And for now, the Museum is a distributed digital archive rather than a building. Its collection can originate anywhere ordinary life is being lived and documented.

It is also one museum and potentially many. Communities may create their own local collections, exhibitions and museum structures using shared infrastructure without surrendering local context or authority.

It is part public-history project, part participatory archive, part artwork, and part experiment in noticing the present from the imagined perspective of the future.

Who makes the collection?

Everyone can. Contributors don't need archival training or a theory of why something matters. The person living with an object often knows something about it that a curator never could.

The Museum preserves contributors’ own words wherever possible and distinguishes archival originals from edited public copies.

Who is behind the Museum?

The Museum was established in 2026 and is currently stewarded as an independent, experimental cultural project rather than a conventional museum organization.

There is no permanent curatorial authority imagined at the center of the Museum. Its longer-term model is distributed stewardship: people and small stewardship groups care for collections, preservation, access, interpretation and exhibitions while the archive itself remains shared across communities.

Technical systems can assist with preservation, cataloging, duplication, translation and access. Human judgment remains central where context, consent, interpretation, memory and disagreement are involved.

AI, data & consent

A large archive of ordinary life is also a dataset about humanity. It may someday help people—and machines— understand how human life actually worked at a level that conventional historical records rarely preserve.

The Museum does not treat that possibility as automatic permission. Public display, research, machine learning, generative reuse, attribution and future unknown uses are separate choices. Contributors should be able to say yes to one and no to another. Public display can also be immediate, delayed until a chosen date or number of years has passed, subject to later confirmation, or declined entirely.

A person can therefore say, in effect: preserve this now, but don't show it yet. Some contributions may remain quietly in the archive for years before becoming public—or may never become public at all. Historical value does not require immediate visibility.

The Museum’s working principle is simple: You may learn from this, but don’t pretend you lived it. Consent, provenance and context belong with the object rather than being stripped away when it becomes data.

What are we trying to preserve?

Context.

The invisible systems underneath an ordinary Tuesday: money, work, housing, technology, transportation, bureaucracy, domestic routines, social rules, waiting, maintenance, care, convenience and inconvenience.

People rarely experience these things as “history” while they are happening. This is exactly why we are interested in them.

The question behind the museum

If someone fifty years from now wanted to understand what it felt like to be alive today, what would we wish we had saved?

Museum information / Stewardship

A museum cared for by more than one person.

The Museum of Ordinary Life is developing a distributed stewardship model in which people can help preserve the collection, shape exhibitions, document ordinary life, maintain its infrastructure, and guide how the Museum evolves.

An invitation to inaugural stewards

We’re looking for a small first group of people to help establish the Museum’s practices—not simply carry out predetermined tasks.

Museum experience is welcome but not required. Archivists, artists, technologists, journalists, librarians, neighborhood historians, careful observers, collectors of strange little things, and anyone attentive to ordinary life may have something to contribute.

Collection

Caring for and describing the collection, reviewing submissions, preserving contributor context, and helping decide what the Museum should collect.

Consent & ethics

Considering privacy, permissions, attribution, delayed release, withdrawal requests, and the difference between preservation, public display, research, machine learning and generative reuse.
