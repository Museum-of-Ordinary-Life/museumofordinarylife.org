One especially close neighbor is the Museum of Everyday Life ↗ in Glover, Vermont, whose philosophy, performances, collections and exhibitions explore people and their relationships to everyday objects.

We discovered their work after establishing the Museum of Ordinary Life in 2026. The similar names were not intentional. The overlap is real and worth acknowledging.

This Museum starts collecting before ordinary context disappears. It keeps objects together with their surroundings, interfaces, routines, documents, sounds, testimony, provenance, permissions, and release dates. The projects overlap, but they aren’t the same project.

Who makes the collection?

Everyone can. Contributors don't need archival training or a theory of why something matters. The person living with an object often knows something about it that a curator never could.

The Museum preserves contributors’ own words wherever possible and distinguishes archival originals from edited public copies.

Who is behind the Museum?

The Museum was established in 2026 and is currently stewarded as an independent experimental cultural project. It’s not yet a conventional museum organization.

The Museum isn’t meant to have a permanent curatorial authority at its center. Its longer-term model is distributed stewardship: people and small stewardship groups care for collections, preservation, access, interpretation and exhibitions while the archive itself remains shared across communities.

Software can handle backups, cataloging, duplication, translation, and access. Questions of context, consent, interpretation, memory, and disagreement remain matters for human judgment.

AI, data & consent

Once the collection is large enough, it also becomes a dataset about human life. Researchers may use it to study details that conventional historical records rarely preserve. Where contributors permit it, the collection may also be used for machine learning.

That possibility doesn’t grant automatic permission. Public display, research, machine learning, generative reuse, attribution, and uses that can’t yet be described are recorded separately. Contributors can say yes to one and no to another. Public display may be immediate, delayed to a date or number of years, made subject to later confirmation, or declined.

A contributor can say: preserve this now, but don’t show it yet. Some records may remain closed for years or permanently. Historical value doesn’t require immediate visibility.

The Museum’s working principle is: You may learn from this, but don’t pretend you lived it. Consent, provenance, and context stay with the object when it becomes data.

What are we trying to preserve?

Context.

The invisible systems underneath an ordinary Tuesday: money, work, housing, technology, transportation, bureaucracy, domestic routines, social rules, waiting, maintenance, care, convenience and inconvenience.

People rarely experience these things as “history” while they are happening. That’s why we’re interested in them.

The record should also leave room for the person: imperfect, adaptive, contradictory, and unfinished. A room, receipt, or routine can document part of a life. It can’t explain the whole life.

The question behind the museum

If someone fifty years from now wanted to understand what it felt like to be alive today, what would we wish we had saved?

Museum information / Stewardship

A museum cared for by more than one person.

The Museum is being built around shared stewardship. Stewards may care for collections, review records, make exhibitions, document ordinary life, maintain the website and preservation systems, and take part in Museum decisions.

An invitation to inaugural stewards

We’re looking for a small first group of people to help establish the Museum’s practices. There’s no finished set of tasks to carry out.

Museum experience is welcome but not required. Archivists, artists, technologists, journalists, librarians, neighborhood historians, careful observers, collectors of strange little things, and anyone attentive to ordinary life may have something to contribute.

Collection

Caring for and describing the collection, reviewing submissions, preserving contributor context, and helping decide what the Museum should collect.

Consent & ethics

Considering privacy, permissions, attribution, delayed release, withdrawal requests, and the difference between preservation, public display, research, machine learning and generative reuse.

Attention & interpretation

Every contribution is a fragment of a person, place, or moment. Stewards should describe what the record supports and stop there; the archive can’t fairly claim more.

The collection documents people; it doesn’t diagnose, rank, explain or reduce them. Description should preserve context without turning ordinary difference, messiness or contradiction into spectacle.

Exhibitions & fieldwork

Developing exhibitions, documenting ordinary environments and routines, and finding ways to help people notice what usually goes unrecorded.

Community

Building relationships with contributors, neighborhoods, local historians, cultural spaces and other communities whose ordinary lives become part of the archive.

Helping communities establish their own local museum structures, preserve culturally specific context, and connect with the wider network only in the ways they choose.

Local museums & shared exhibitions

Supporting place-based, community-based and thematic museums that remain locally shaped while sharing preservation tools, documentation and mutual support.

Exhibitions may be local, collaborative across several communities, or Museum-wide. Each record keeps its provenance and permissions.

Infrastructure & continuity

Keeping the website, backups, preservation copies, domains, accessibility work, and operating documentation in order so the Museum can outlive any one steward.

What participation means

Stewards help shape the institution from the beginning. Participation can start with one conversation or gathering; there is no expectation of an ongoing commitment.

The first stewardship circle will set practical rules for decisions, access, documentation, succession, and the handoff of responsibilities.

Project context / beneath the museum

This museum exists in two times at once.

The Museum of Ordinary Life is a real archive established in 2026. It’s also an institution imagined inside a larger speculative fiction project called No One Noticed.

The larger project

No One Noticed began as a speculative fiction project about a near-future social and technological change that happens gradually enough that the people living through it don’t recognize a single moment when the world changes.

The project now includes a book, websites, archival experiments, art, design, software, and real artifacts made from its fictional future.

Why make the Museum real?

In the fictional future, the Museum is a source on ordinary life before and during that change. The real Museum starts the collection now instead of inventing it later, while the period it may someday describe is still the present.

A photograph made in 2026 can be contemporary documentation, art, historical evidence, and an artifact within the fiction at the same time.

The reality layer

The Museum is the first part of a wider “Reality Layer”: websites, archives, tools, interfaces, documents, and institutions from the imagined future that can be built in the present.

Some will remain fictional. Some may become working prototypes. A few may become useful institutions on their own.

The archive becomes a dataset

Collect enough ordinary life and the archive becomes a machine-readable record of human routines, objects, spaces, and explanations that conventional datasets often miss.

In No One Noticed, future intelligent systems use the Museum to study what people valued, how they lived, and what they chose to preserve. The real Museum sets rules for that use now.

Permissions are part of the record. They document how people wanted their lives represented, studied, and learned from.

Some records may open years after they were contributed. An item documented in 2026 might remain private until 2036, 2046, or later. Over time, the Museum may hold unopened time capsules whose release instructions are part of the archive.
