Nothing was ordinary
while someone was living it.

An archive of ordinary objects, rooms, interfaces, routines, paperwork, sounds, stories, and the context around them.

We began collecting in 2026 while the context around ordinary things could still be recorded. We don’t yet know which parts of the present will be difficult to explain later.

We collect the things people might never think to preserve.

Why ordinary life?

Much of the present will disappear without leaving a record.

01

The object

The receipt, key, charger, badge, bottle, bag, cable, or handwritten note. The object is evidence; its context belongs in the record too.

02

The context

Where it was kept, how it was used, what surrounded it, and what those details explain.

03

The voice

“I use this every day.” “I hate this thing.” “I don’t know why I still have it.” Ordinary testimony is part of the collection.

Attention is part of the archive

Archives usually preserve what someone thought worth saving. Ordinary life is easier to lose: bedrooms, grocery lists, interfaces, routines, meals, workplaces, conversations, clutter, sounds, habits, and the small arrangements people build around themselves.

Ordinary moments don’t need hidden greatness to belong here. A human life doesn’t have to be exceptional to matter.

To pay attention to ordinary life is to practice seeing people as fully human.

Attention here means looking carefully without turning a record into a verdict. A photograph, room, receipt, or routine can document part of a life. It can’t contain the whole person.

One museum / Local collections

Ordinary life is everywhere. Its stories should be, too.

The Museum may take many local forms. A neighborhood, town, language community, diaspora, workplace, or temporary group can keep its own collection and still be part of the wider Museum.
