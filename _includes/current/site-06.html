A record is partial. Document what is there without pretending it explains the person. A room, receipt, routine, or group of objects can show context while the person remains larger and more complicated than the record.

Archive ethics / Data & AI

A record of humanity shouldn’t become a free-for-all dataset.

Once the collection is large enough, it can also function as a dataset about people: how they lived, what they kept, how homes and streets worked, what software looked like, what worried them, and what an ordinary Tuesday felt like.

The collection may be useful for research, art, data analysis, and machine learning. That doesn’t make every use permitted. Public display, research, machine learning, and generative reuse are recorded as separate permissions.

Preservation and publication are separate decisions. A contribution can enter the archive without entering public view. Contributors may choose immediate display, anonymous display, release on or after a date, a delay measured in years, a later request for permission, or archival preservation without public display.

A dataset is still a partial record. It may show patterns, surroundings, or habits, but it doesn’t contain the whole person who contributed it. Search and computation don’t erase the need for human context.

Museum information / About

A museum for the things we haven’t lost yet.

Established in 2026, the Museum of Ordinary Life documents everyday life while its objects, routines, interfaces, and explanations are still available. We collect ordinary things together with the context that makes them legible.

What we do

We collect and document ordinary objects, spaces, interfaces, routines, sounds, paperwork, and personal accounts that would otherwise leave little historical record.

The Museum records the thing and its place in a life: where it was kept, how it was used, what surrounded it, and what the person living with it thought about it. The object is evidence; the surrounding life is part of the record.

Why 2026?

Because this is when we started.

Historical collections are often assembled after the fact, when ordinary details have already disappeared. We’re documenting the present before we know what will later seem important, strange, obsolete, beautiful, or difficult to explain.

What counts?

A grocery receipt can count. So can a bus stop, a work badge, a phone notification, a medicine cabinet, a rent notice, a junk drawer, a five-minute recording of a street, or the place somebody charges their phone every night.

Rarity is not a requirement. Neither are beauty or a profound story. A common object may tell a future researcher more than a rare one.

Why ordinary life?

Archives usually preserve what someone thought worth saving. Ordinary life is easier to lose: bedrooms, grocery lists, interfaces, routines, meals, workplaces, conversations, clutter, sounds, habits, and the small arrangements people build around themselves.

Ordinary moments don’t need hidden greatness to belong here. A human life doesn’t have to be exceptional to matter.

To pay attention to ordinary life is to practice seeing people as fully human.

That also means resisting the urge to turn one contribution into a verdict about the person behind it. The archive can preserve evidence of a life without pretending that any object, photograph or description contains the whole person.

What kind of museum is this?

For now, the Museum is a distributed digital archive, not a building. Records can originate anywhere ordinary life is being lived and documented.

The Museum may also take local forms. Communities may create their own local collections, exhibitions and museum structures using shared preservation and cataloging tools while keeping local context and authority.

The Museum borrows methods from public history, participatory archives, and art. Its first obligation is archival: preserve records with enough context that someone else can understand them later, while the people and systems that produced them are still here to describe them.
