Consent is history too

How people choose to share—or not share—their lives with future humans and machines is itself part of the historical record.

These permissions describe the Museum’s intended stewardship model. Some forms of downstream copying, indexing or previously completed model training may not be technically reversible; where that is true, the Museum should say so plainly rather than promise control it cannot provide. A delayed-release or archive-only instruction governs public access through the Museum; it does not silently grant research, machine-learning, generative-reuse or other permissions.

Contribute / Opening collection

Send us something boring.

Photograph or record something from your ordinary life in 2026. Boring is exciting. There is no minimum level of historical significance. That is, in part, the entire point.

01Send the original photo, video, audio file, screenshot or document when possible (sans anything with personal or private information of course).
02Tell us what it is and when you documented it.
03Give an approximate location—as broad as you prefer (a city name is more than enough).
04Add any context you want. One sentence is enough. No explanation is also useful information.
05 Choose when and how it may appear publicly: "publish now with my name" / "publish now anonymously" / "publish on or after [date]" / "wait [X years] before publishing" / "ask me again after [date or X years]" / "archive only and ask before publishing" / "archive only—don't publish."
06 Tell us how the contribution may be used beyond display. You can separately allow or decline research, machine learning, generative reuse, attribution, and future unknown uses—or ask us to contact you again. These choices can be more restrictive than your publication choice.

Please don't send private information belonging to other people without their permission. Public copies may be cropped, resized or redacted; archival originals are kept separately. Publication permission does not automatically grant permission for research, machine learning or generative reuse. The Museum should preserve those choices with the contribution and honor later changes where technically and legally feasible. Preserving something and publishing it are different decisions: a delayed-release contribution can remain privately archived until the contributor’s chosen date or interval has passed, and an “ask again” instruction is not permission to publish automatically when that time arrives.

Museum information / About

A museum for the things we haven’t lost yet.

The Museum of Ordinary Life is a living archive of everyday human life, established in 2026 while the material it collects is still ordinary.

What we do

We document ordinary objects, spaces, interfaces, routines, sounds, paperwork and personal accounts that usually pass through the world without entering a historical record.

The Museum is interested not only in what something looked like, but in how it fit into a life: where it lived, how it was used, what systems surrounded it and what the person living with it thought about it.

Why 2026?

Because this is when we started.

Historical archives are often assembled after the fact, when the mundane details of an era have already disappeared. We are trying the opposite: documenting the present before we know what will eventually seem important, strange, obsolete, beautiful or difficult to explain.

What counts?

A grocery receipt can count. So can a bus stop, a work badge, a phone notification, a medicine cabinet, a rent notice, a junk drawer, a five-minute recording of a street, or the place somebody charges their phone every night.

Rarity isn't a requirement. Neither is beauty or a profound story. Ubiquity may be more historically useful than uniqueness.
