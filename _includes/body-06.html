Project context / beneath the museum

This museum exists in two times at once.

The Museum of Ordinary Life is a real archive established in 2026. It is also an institution imagined inside a larger speculative-fiction project called No One Noticed.

The larger project

No One Noticed began as a speculative-fiction project about a near-future societal and technological transformation that happens so gradually—and so seamlessly— that the people living through it don't recognize a singular moment when the world changes.

The project has since expanded beyond a book into a broader experiment in worldbuilding, archival practice, art, design, software, social imagination and the creation of real artifacts from a fictional future.

Why make the Museum real?

In the fictional future, the Museum helps people understand how ordinary life worked before and during the transition. Rather than inventing that historical collection later, the real Museum begins collecting now—while the world it will someday remember is still the present.

A real photograph made in 2026 can therefore function simultaneously as contemporary documentation, an artwork, historical evidence, and an artifact inside the fictional continuity.

The reality layer

The Museum is the first part of a wider “Reality Layer.” Websites, archives, tools, interfaces, documents, institutions and other fragments of the imagined future that can exist meaningfully in the present.

Some may remain fictional artifacts or become functioning prototypes. Others may become actually useful institutions independent of the story that inspired them.

The archive becomes a dataset

There is another consequence to collecting ordinary life at scale: the Museum becomes a remarkably detailed, machine-readable record of humanity—one rich in the mundane details that conventional datasets often miss.

In the world of No One Noticed, that creates a useful tension. Future intelligent systems may learn from the Museum when trying to understand what people valued, how they lived and what was worth preserving. The real Museum therefore treats the ethics of that future use as something to design now rather than discover later.

The permissions attached to the collection become part of the collection too: a record not only of human life, but of how people wanted human life to be represented, studied and learned from.

Some records may also arrive from the past on a schedule chosen by the person who contributed them. An item documented in 2026 might remain private until 2036, 2046 or later, when its contributor decided it could finally enter public view. The Museum can therefore accumulate not only artifacts, but unopened time capsules whose release instructions are themselves part of the archive.
